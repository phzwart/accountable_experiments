# 5 Results (draft 3)

*All numbers below come from the run record: 36 configurations and nine comparisons, each run as 250 campaigns with independent calibration draws (SI 5).*


## 5.1 The guarantee holds

Checking a probability bound needs repeated campaigns against a known truth. Every certified level of every campaign was therefore checked against the fully measured truth (SI 5).

The picked failure probabilities $\delta_L$ (Section 3.3) averaged 0.19 against a nominal 0.25 for the IR map and 0.11 to 0.12 against 0.20 for the NaCl and synthetic maps. The observed exceedances are to be compared with $\delta_L$, and the four levels of a NaCl campaign hold together with probability at least about one half.

The record agrees with the guarantee at the picked failure probabilities: over the 32 statements whose budget can be exceeded on every level, observed exceedances lay between 0.88 and 1.11 times the number expected, and none exceeded it beyond chance (SI 5, Table S5.3). On the IR map at its original tolerance, three of the four levels hold too few failing sites for the budget to be reached. Repeating the calibration draw on the recorded level populations gives the same picture (Table S5.2). Against a fixed truth these counts check the rule and its implementation (Section 6).

A plan can be certified for all levels together by dividing its failure probability among its levels when it is declared. Certified level by level, the plans exceeded the budget on at least one level in 20 %, 38 % and 50 % of campaigns on the IR, NaCl and synthetic maps. Certified for all levels together, at confidence 0.75 for IR and 0.80 for the other two, they measured 415 ± 82, 694 ± 58 and 377 ± 27 sites, 41 %, 13 % and 13 % more, and exceeded it in 1, 24 and 20 of 250 campaigns, within the 25 % and 20 % the contracts allow (SI 5.3). Their 1, 27 and 20 level checks over budget compare with 30, 28 and 23 expected under the picked failure probabilities.

Conformal risk control on the same draw bounds only the expected share of a level skipped wrongly [Angelopoulos2024]. It measured 260, 542 and 305 sites. Its mean share of a level skipped wrongly, 5.7 %, 18.8 % and 18.3 %, stayed below the budget, as promised; single levels exceeded it in 5 %, 35 % and 32 % of checks, and campaigns on at least one level in 20 %, 81 % and 92 % (SI 5.3).

Breaking the order of operations breaks the guarantee (Section 3.1). On a simulated level of 399 sites with a budget of 0.10 and a picked failure probability of 0.206, the calibration draw was repeated 4000 times under several rules (SI 5.2). Scores frozen before a uniform draw exceeded the budget in 20.5 % of draws, as did a score that knew the true labels: what the score knows changes the cost and leaves the guarantee intact. Rules that use the draw broke it, in every draw when the scores of failing calibration sites were raised afterwards, in 63 % when calibration sites were taken where the scores were least sure, as an active learner would, and in 69 % when the best of five draws was kept. Setting one threshold on the calibration sites the other left uncovered exceeded the budget in 28.8 % of draws, where the priority path, which fixes the same preference in advance, stayed at 20.5 %. Every form of Table 1 kept its guarantee, and none was the least expensive on all three maps (Fig. S3; Table S3.1).

## 5.2 A model's own error rate is checked only against the full scan

Decisions resting on a model's stated error rate need that rate to hold. On the NaCl scan, a Gaussian process refitted after each level of the same fixed design skipped a site when it was sure the line was absent, or when its 68 % interval on the width lay within the tolerance (Fig. 1; SI 7). Its statement, the model's own probability of a wrong skip summed over its skips, can be set against the realised share of the level, the quantity the guarantee bounds. With the latent standard deviation it measured 658 of 1071 sites, stated 3.5 % of the certified-level sites wrong and skipped 10.4 % wrongly; at 90 % to 99 % intervals it stated 0.4 % to 0.5 % and realised 1.0 % to 1.4 %. With the predictive standard deviation, which adds the fitted noise, no interval of the width fell within the tolerance; every skip was for want of a line, and the process stated 0.4 % and realised 0.3 %, at 922 measurements. The certified plans measured 614 ± 56 sites with natural-neighbour interpolation and 745 to 777 with the Gaussian process as predictor, and their bound held at the stated confidence (Fig. S7). Certifying the Gaussian process thus cost about 100 measurements more than trusting its latent intervals and saved about 150 against its predictive ones.

The comparison was repeated on all three maps with the Gaussian process of the certified campaigns in Section 5.4, which predicts the whole profile. A site was skipped under a claim when the posterior probability of a wrong skip was at most α, for α from 0.01 to 0.32, with the latent standard deviation, the predictive one, or the predictive one rescaled to the leave-one-out residuals of the measured sites (SI 7). Stated and realised shares of wrong skips disagreed in either direction (Fig. 4). On the synthetic map the process stated at most 4 % and skipped 11 % to 41 % of the certified-level sites wrongly. Rescaling made it worse, and the reason is the one a uniform draw removes: the sites the process chose to measure were easier for it to predict than those it left out, and the scale fell to between 0.23 and 0.87 of its value. On IR the stated shares stayed below 10 % and the realised ones below 5 %, with 73 to 398 of 959 sites measured. On NaCl the process judged almost every site too uncertain to skip and measured 959 to 1070 of 1071.

The misstatement lay in the interpolation claim. On the synthetic map, sites skipped only because the line was judged absent were almost never wrong, 0 to 2 of 293 to 509; every other wrong skip failed the tolerance (SI 7). A rate stated by the model can thus be right or several times too low, and only sites the model did not choose show which (Section 5.6). The certified campaigns at budgets from 0.05 to 0.30 realised on average less than their bound on every map; certified points pair a bound with a mean, uncertified points a stated expectation with a realisation.

## 5.3 Every action certified

An instrument record becomes useful once every site of a map carries its reason. Figure 5 shows one campaign of average cost per dataset under the quantile path with natural-neighbour interpolation, with every design site marked by the reason recorded for it and every wrong skip found against the fully measured truth.

In the campaigns shown, the wrong skips were 3.8 % of the sites on certified levels of the IR map, against a budget of 0.10 per level, and 11.8 % on NaCl and 11.6 % on the synthetic map, against 0.20. The wrong skips of the synthetic map gather in two compact regions of the composition triangle; the guarantee bounds their rate on each level and leaves their location open (Section 5.5; SI 6).

In hindsight, the record shows which measurements every claim would have skipped wrongly (SI 8): 45 % of the sites measured for want of a claim on the synthetic map, against 10 % of sites drawn at random, and 47 % against 35 % on NaCl, where the width scatters from site to site by about the tolerance.

## 5.4 The model sets the cost

Freedom in the choice of model has a price, paid in measurements. The quantile path was run with four interpolation protocols on each dataset at the nominal guarantees of Section 5.1: natural-neighbour interpolation with the logistic score, and Gaussian-process interpolation with the posterior standard deviation as score and three rules for the length scale (Section 4.3). At the original IR tolerance almost every site is interpolable and the interest claim alone sets the cost; at the tight tolerance interpolation decides.

A score that carries no information still yields a valid guarantee, at a price. A random scorer, an independent uniform weight per site and claim, exceeded the budget no more often than expected on every map and cost 1.4 to 3.2 times as many measurements as informed scores, 716 ± 71 against 295 ± 53 of 959 sites on IR (Fig. 6). The guarantee comes from the calibration draw; the score decides what it costs.

Every guarantee held, with exceedance counts on each map close to those expected under the picked failure probabilities and none beyond chance (Fig. 6; Table S5.3).

The cost depends on the protocol and on the data (Fig. 6; Table S3.2). At the original IR tolerance the four protocols measured 273 ± 31 to 295 ± 53 of 959 sites with skipped sets overlapping by about 90 %; at the tight tolerance they measured 621 ± 70 to 799 ± 40, and the Gaussian-process protocols shared a quarter to a third of their skips with natural-neighbour interpolation. On NaCl, the Gaussian-process protocols measured 745 to 777 of 1071 sites, against 614 ± 56. On the synthetic map they measured between 1040 ± 202 and 1443 ± 133 of 2118 sites, against 334 ± 26 and 1076 ± 187 with random scores; their score, the posterior standard deviation at unit output scale, depends on the positions of the measured sites and the length scale only. The difference arises in the calibration draws: more failing calibration sites admit fewer skips at the same budget, and the guarantee is unchanged.

A certified plan has to earn its place against a uniform sample of the same size. With every other site predicted from all measured sites, such a sample left 0.5 ± 0.2 %, 22.0 ± 1.0 % and 10.6 ± 1.0 % of the certified-level sites outside tolerance on the IR, NaCl and synthetic maps; the final maps of the certified plans, predicted in the same way, left 0.4 ± 0.3 %, 18.0 ± 3.8 % and 8.4 ± 1.4 % (SI 5.3). The contract sets the rest: on the IR map at confidence 0.80, calibration samples of 20, 40 and 80 sites per level cost 340 ± 90, 337 ± 63 and 393 ± 22 measurements, and at 0.95 a sample of 20 certified no level (SI 10.4).

## 5.5 The claim decides what is protected

Each claim protects what it states, so its choice deserves a record. Six acceptance metrics were compared on the three maps with the interpolation claim alone (SI 6). Every metric kept its guarantee, and the metric set the cost: at tolerances a practitioner might pick, the IR map needed between 233 and 810 measurements. At tolerances matched to equal difficulty the metric still decided where measurements went, with the peak claim on the synthetic map measuring where the peak drifts with composition and the profile claim along the phase boundaries (Fig. S6), and it decided where wrong skips fell. The rate counts wrong skips over all sites of a level; on the synthetic map 35 % of the interesting sites on certified levels were skipped in error while every level met its budget (SI 6.2).

The label-conditional statement of Section 3.4 closes that gap. At a share of 0.20 and confidence 0.80, calibrated on 12 to 20 sites of interest per level, the plan lost 8.5 ± 4.4 % of the interesting sites, against 34.7 ± 8.5 %, at 359 ± 26 measurements against 334 ± 26; 4.9 % of level checks exceeded the share, against 58.7 % without it (SI 10.1).

## 5.6 Checks on the finished map

An audit of 100 skipped sites after the last level (Section 3.6) bounded, at confidence 0.80, the share of certified-level sites that the final map predicts outside tolerance at 1.5 %, 19.8 % and 11.1 % on the IR, NaCl and synthetic maps, against 0.4 %, 18.0 % and 8.4 %, and held in 100 %, 83 % and 85 % of audit draws (SI 10.3). A campaign steered by its own model meets a budget only when the model's statement holds. On the NaCl scan a Gaussian process measured, batch by batch, the sites at which it judged a skip most likely to be wrong, and stopped once its stated share of certified-level sites predicted wrongly reached 0.20 (SI 10.3). With the latent standard deviation it stopped at the 76 seed sites, its map wrong at 36.8 % of the sites, in all of 30 runs. With the predictive one it stopped after 451 to 501 measurements at 14.6 ± 0.6 %, fewer than the certified plan needed; the same statement fell short by factors of 3 to 10 on the synthetic map (Section 5.2). An audit of 100 sites at the stopping point told the two cases apart, confirming the budget in 89 % of draws for the predictive rule and in none for the latent one.

Drift was simulated as a slow swelling of the synthetic sample, its $q$ scale stretched in proportion to the number of sites measured before (SI 10.2). The bound on the skips held, since drifted calibration sites disagree with earlier predictions and the rule skips less: at a stretch of 10 % the plan measured 468 ± 79 sites against 330 ± 26. The error moved into the measurements, 21 % of which lay outside tolerance of the sample at the start, where no claim applies. Five seed sites measured again after each level flagged 98 % of these campaigns and none without drift.
