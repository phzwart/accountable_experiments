# 5 Results (draft 3)

*All numbers below come from the run record: 36 configurations, each run as 250 independent campaigns with their own calibration draws (SI 5).*

## 5.1 The guarantee holds

A guarantee is worth only as much as its record of being kept. Every certified level of every campaign was therefore checked against the fully measured truth (SI 5).

The failure probability a level actually carries is set by the calibration sample. The rule allows $k$ failing calibration sites, and the worst-case probability of exceeding the budget at that $k$ takes only a few values. The rule picks the largest of these values at or below the nominal $\delta$, and that picked value $\delta_L$ is the failure probability the level guarantees. On the levels of this study, $\delta_L$ averaged 0.19 against a nominal 0.25 for the IR map and 0.11 to 0.12 against 0.20 for the NaCl and synthetic maps. The observed exceedances are to be compared with $\delta_L$.

The record agrees with the guarantee at the picked levels. For the 32 stated rates (configuration and statement) whose budget can be exceeded on every level, union-bound statements excluded, the observed number of exceedances lay between 0.88 and 1.11 times the number the picked levels allow, and none exceeded it beyond chance: the smallest one-sided probability of the observed count under the picked levels was 0.10 (SI 5, Table S5.3). On the IR map at its original tolerance, three of the four levels hold too few failing sites for the budget to be reached, and the record shows no exceedance there. The union-bound statement of the per-claim form is exceeded far less often than its picked level allows, since two claims seldom approach their budgets on the same level. Repeating the calibration draw on the recorded level populations gives the same picture: pooled over the levels of each configuration, the attained exceedance probability lay between 0.94 and 1.02 times $\delta_L$ wherever the budget could be exceeded (SI 5, Table S5.2).

A score that carries no information leaves the guarantee intact. A random scorer gave every site an independent uniform weight for every certificate. The shared rate exceeded its budget in 168 of 1000 level checks on IR, 194 of 1000 on IR at the tight tolerance, 127 of 1000 on NaCl and 161 of 1500 on the synthetic map, against 25 %, 25 %, 20 % and 20 % allowed (Fig. 5, lower row). The random order cost 1.4 to 3.2 times as many measurements as informed scores, 716 ± 71 against 295 ± 53 of 959 sites on IR (Fig. 5, upper row). The guarantee comes from the calibration draw; the score decides what it costs.

Breaking the order of operations breaks the guarantee (Section 3.1). On a level of 399 sites with informative scores, a budget of 0.10 and a picked failure probability of 0.206, the calibration draw was repeated 4000 times under five rules. With scores frozen before a uniform draw, the budget was exceeded in 822 draws (0.205). A score that knew the true labels but was frozen before the draw gave 819 (0.205): what the score knows changes the cost and leaves the guarantee intact. Raising the scores of the failing calibration sites after the draw and thresholding on the same labels exceeded the budget in every draw. Taking the calibration sample from the half of the level the scores rank as least safe, as an active learner would, gave 2502 exceedances (0.63), and keeping the most favourable of five draws gave 2771 (0.69).

The shared rate exceeded its budget in 0, 49 and 49 of 1000 level checks for the per-claim form, the quantile path and the priority path on IR, in 9, 112 and 123 of 1000 on NaCl, and in 5, 161 and 156 of 1500 on the synthetic map. No form was the least expensive on all three maps: the per-claim form measured 262 ± 28 of 959 sites on IR against 295 ± 53 for the quantile path, the quantile path 614 ± 56 of 1071 on NaCl against 678 to 719, and on the synthetic map the priority path cost almost twice as much as the other two. The sequential rule of the original IR analysis, which sets the interpolation threshold on the calibration sites left uncovered by the interest threshold, exceeded $R_E = 0.10$ in 28.8 % of 4000 draws against 25 % allowed; the priority path, which fixes the same preference in advance, did so in 20.5 %.

## 5.2 The model sets the cost

A guarantee is of use only if it holds whichever model proposes the skips, and its cost then shows what the model is worth. The quantile path was run with four interpolation protocols on each dataset at the nominal guarantees of Section 5.1: natural-neighbour interpolation with the logistic score, and Gaussian-process interpolation with the posterior standard deviation as score and three rules for the length scale (Section 4.3). At the original IR tolerance almost every site is interpolable and the interest claim alone sets the cost; at the tight tolerance interpolation decides.

Every guarantee held, with exceedance counts on each map within those the picked levels allow (Fig. 6, lower row).

The cost depends on the protocol and on the data (Fig. 6, upper row; Table S3.2). At the original IR tolerance the four protocols measured 273 ± 31 to 295 ± 53 of 959 sites with skipped sets overlapping by about 90 %; at the tight tolerance they measured 621 ± 70 to 799 ± 40, and the Gaussian-process protocols shared a quarter to a third of their skips with natural-neighbour interpolation. On NaCl, the Gaussian-process protocols measured 745 to 777 of 1071 sites, against 614 ± 56. On the synthetic map they measured between 1040 ± 202 and 1443 ± 133 of 2118 sites, against 334 ± 26. The difference arises in the calibration draws: more failing calibration sites admit fewer skips at the same budget, and the guarantee is unchanged.

## 5.3 An uncertified agent cannot price its errors

A guarantee earns its cost where the model's own uncertainty cannot supply the same statement. On the NaCl scan, a Gaussian process refitted after each level skipped a site when it was sure the line was absent, or when its 68 % interval on the width lay within the tolerance (Fig. 1; SI 7). It measured 658 of 1071 sites and skipped 10 % of the certified-level sites wrongly, inside the spread of the certified campaigns, which measured 614 ± 56 sites with natural-neighbour interpolation and 745 to 777 with the Gaussian process as predictor. Certifying the Gaussian process cost about 100 more measurements and bought the statement: the uncertified rate went unstated and fell unevenly, 18 % on the last level, and intervals of 90 % to 99 % allowed 21 to 31 predictable skips, of which 29 % to 32 % were wrong against the 1 % to 10 % the intervals imply.

The comparison was repeated on all three maps with the Gaussian process of the certified campaigns in Section 5.2, which predicts the whole profile. A site was skipped under a claim when the posterior probability of a wrong skip was at most α, for α from 0.01 to 0.32, with the latent standard deviation, the predictive one, or the predictive one rescaled to the leave-one-out residuals of the measured sites (SI 7). Stated and realised shares of wrong skips disagreed in either direction (Fig. 7, upper row). On the synthetic map the process stated at most 4 % and skipped 11 % to 41 % of the certified-level sites wrongly. Rescaling made it worse: the measured sites were easier to predict than the skipped ones, and the scale fell to between 0.23 and 0.87 of its value. On IR the stated shares stayed below 10 % and the realised ones below 5 %, with 73 to 398 of 959 sites measured. On NaCl the process judged almost every site too uncertain to skip and measured 959 to 1070 of 1071.

A misstated rate carries a price whenever decisions rest on it. For a cost ratio r of a wrong skip to a measurement, each agent took the operating point that minimised the cost it believed, the measurements plus r times the wrong skips it stated, with the bound as the statement of a certified campaign, and was charged the cost it incurred (Fig. 7, lower row). On average the certified campaigns never paid more than they expected. With natural-neighbour interpolation they stayed within 1.1 times the cheapest available option on NaCl and 1.6 times on the synthetic map at every ratio; on IR the calibration draws, 160 of 959 sites, made them up to 4.5 times dearer than the uncertified process for ratios below 13, the fixed price of the statement. The uncertified process paid up to 2.1 times the cheapest option on NaCl. On the synthetic map it paid 4 to 6 times that option at ratios up to 10 and 107 to 158 times at 1000, up to 667 times what it expected, and on IR its bill reached 7 to 15 times the cheapest option at a ratio of 1000. The price of a misstated rate grows with the cost of a wrong skip, and its size and direction showed only against the full scans.

## 5.4 Every action certified

A guarantee on rates becomes useful at an instrument once every site of a map carries its reason. Figure 8 shows one campaign per dataset under the quantile path with natural-neighbour interpolation, with every design site marked by the reason recorded for it and every wrong skip found against the fully measured truth.

In the campaigns shown, the wrong skips were 2.3 % of the sites on certified levels of the IR map, against a budget of 0.10 per level, and 13.3 % on NaCl and 10.3 % on the synthetic map, against 0.20. The wrong skips of the synthetic map gather in two compact regions of the composition triangle; the guarantee bounds their rate on each level and leaves their location open (Section 5.5).

The map bound states, from the calibration draw of a level, how many of its sites the map built from coarser levels predicts outside tolerance. Over the 250 campaigns of each configuration it held on 100 % of the certified levels for IR, 88.5 % for NaCl and 90.4 % for the synthetic map, against confidences of 0.75, 0.80 and 0.80. On NaCl about half of the last-level sites lie outside that tolerance; the skip guarantee concerns only the sites a certificate covers.

The record also shows whether a measurement was needed, that is, whether every claim would have skipped the site wrongly (SI 8). Among sites measured because no claim covered them, 45 % were needed on the synthetic map against 10 % of sites drawn at random, and 47 % against 35 % on NaCl, where the width scatters from site to site by about the tolerance.

## 5.5 The claim decides what is protected

A guarantee covers the claim it is given, so the choice of claim needs its own record. Six acceptance metrics were compared on the three maps with the interpolation certificate alone and 50 campaigns per metric: peak or line descriptors with absolute or relative tolerance, two forms of profile envelope, and the error in a basis of the seed spectra, plain or with near-duplicate spectra merged (SI 6). Every metric kept its guarantee. Over 32 combinations of metric, dataset and tolerance, the largest ratio of observed to allowed exceedances was 1.37, with a one-sided probability of 0.04 under the picked levels; all other ratios were at most 1.08.

The metric set the cost. At tolerances a practitioner might pick, the share of certified-level sites that a prediction fails ranged from 1 % to 85 %, and the IR map needed between 233 and 810 measurements. Scaling every tolerance so that 15 % of the sites fail, a control that uses the truth, brought the costs close: 338–453 sites on IR, 287–367 on NaCl and 398–461 on the synthetic map.

At equal difficulty the metric still decided where measurements go (Fig. 9). On the synthetic map the peak claim measured where the peak position and width drift with composition, in the micellar phase and in a band of the emulsion. The profile claim measured along the phase boundaries: 45–46 % of its measurements fell on the 8.8 % of sites next to another phase, against 32 % for the peak claim. The two claims placed 31 % of their measurements on the same sites, and 16 % at the default tolerances.

The metric also decided where wrong skips fall. Metrics with an absolute term put 72–83 % of the IR wrong skips on sites with signal, 30 % of the skipped sites, and the NaCl line-width claim put 6 % of its wrong skips on sites holding the line, 66 % of the skipped sites. Under every metric the wrong skips clustered, with another wrong skip among the six nearest neighbours 1.5 to 3.0 times as often as for wrong skips placed at random among the skipped sites. The rate also counts wrong skips over all sites of a level. On the synthetic map, where a third of the sites are of interest, 35 % of the interesting sites on certified levels were skipped in error while every level met its budget (SI 6.2).
