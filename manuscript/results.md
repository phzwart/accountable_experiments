# 5 Results (draft 3)

*All numbers below come from the run record: 36 configurations, each run as 250 independent campaigns with their own calibration draws (SI 5).*

## 5.1 The guarantee holds

A guarantee is worth only as much as its record of being kept. Every certified level of every campaign was therefore checked against the fully measured truth (SI 5).

The failure probability a level actually carries is set by the calibration sample. The rule allows $k$ failing calibration sites, and the worst-case probability of exceeding the budget at that $k$ takes only a few values: with 40 calibration sites and a budget of 0.10 on a level of 428 sites, allowing 2 failures gives 0.206 and allowing 3 gives 0.410. The rule picks the largest of these values at or below the nominal $\delta$, and that picked value $\delta_L$ is the failure probability the level guarantees. On the levels of this study, $\delta_L$ averaged 0.19 against a nominal 0.25 for the IR map and 0.11 to 0.12 against 0.20 for the NaCl and synthetic maps. The observed exceedances are to be compared with $\delta_L$.

The record agrees with the guarantee at the picked levels. For the 32 stated rates (configuration and statement) whose budget can be exceeded on every level, union-bound statements excluded, the observed number of exceedances lay between 0.88 and 1.11 times the number the picked levels allow, and none exceeded it beyond chance: the smallest one-sided probability of the observed count under the picked levels was 0.10 (SI 5, Table S5.3). On the IR map at its original tolerance, three of the four levels hold too few failing sites for the budget to be reached, and the record shows no exceedance there. The union-bound statement of the per-claim form is exceeded far less often than its picked level allows, since two claims seldom approach their budgets on the same level. Repeating the calibration draw on the recorded level populations gives the same picture: pooled over the levels of each configuration, the attained exceedance probability lay between 0.94 and 1.02 times $\delta_L$ wherever the budget could be exceeded (SI 5, Table S5.2).

Table 4 gives one configuration per map: what was measured, why each site was skipped, and how the record compares with the guarantee. The guarantee bounds wrong skips as a share of all sites of a level; among the skipped sites alone the share is larger, up to 26.7 % on the NaCl scan.

A score that carries no information leaves the guarantee intact. A random scorer gave every site an independent uniform weight for every certificate. The shared rate exceeded its budget in 168 of 1000 level checks on IR, 194 of 1000 on IR at the tight tolerance, 127 of 1000 on NaCl and 161 of 1500 on the synthetic map, against 25 %, 25 %, 20 % and 20 % allowed (Fig. 5, lower row). The random order cost measurements: 716 ± 71 of 959 sites on IR against 295 ± 53 with informed scores, 871 ± 31 against 621 ± 70 at the tight tolerance, 861 ± 50 of 1071 against 614 ± 56 on NaCl, and 1076 ± 187 of 2118 against 334 ± 26 on the synthetic map (Fig. 5, upper row). The guarantee comes from the calibration draw; the score decides what it costs.

Breaking the order of operations breaks the guarantee (Section 3.1). On a level of 399 sites with informative scores, a budget of 0.10 and a picked failure probability of 0.206, the calibration draw was repeated 4000 times under five rules. With scores frozen before a uniform draw, the budget was exceeded in 822 draws (0.205). A score that knew the true labels but was frozen before the draw gave 819 (0.205): what the score knows changes the cost and leaves the guarantee intact. Raising the scores of the failing calibration sites after the draw and thresholding on the same labels exceeded the budget in every draw. Taking the calibration sample from the half of the level the scores rank as least safe, as an active learner would, gave 2502 exceedances (0.63), and keeping the most favourable of five draws gave 2771 (0.69).

## 5.2 Every form keeps its guarantee

A choice between forms of guarantee needs their costs on real data. Each form of Table 1 was run on the three maps at one nominal guarantee on the shared event: $R_E \le 0.10$ at confidence 0.75 for the IR map, $R_E \le 0.20$ at confidence 0.80 for the NaCl scan and the synthetic map. The per-claim form reached that guarantee through a split, 0.10 at confidence 0.90 per claim for NaCl and the synthetic map, 0.05 at confidence 0.875 for IR. All runs used exact natural-neighbour interpolation and the logistic scores of Section 4.

Every stated rate held (Fig. 6, lower row). The shared rate exceeded its budget in 0, 49 and 49 of 1000 level checks for the three IR forms, in 9, 112 and 123 of 1000 for NaCl, and in 5, 161 and 156 of 1500 for the synthetic map. The per-claim guarantees of the split form exceeded their budgets in 37 and 0 of 1000 checks on IR (12.5 % allowed), 64 and 57 of 1000 on NaCl and 73 and 67 of 1500 on the synthetic map (10 % allowed). The shared guarantee of the split form, obtained through the union bound, was almost never exceeded: two claims seldom approach their budgets on the same level.

The forms differ in cost, and the ordering changes between datasets (Fig. 6, upper row; Table S3.1). On IR the per-claim form measured 262 ± 28 of 959 sites, the quantile path 295 ± 53 and the priority path 327 ± 80. The per-claim split is feasible on IR only through the exact finite-population tail, which admits zero calibration failures at a budget of 0.05 where the binomial approximation admits none. On NaCl, the quantile path was the least expensive form (614 ± 56 of 1071 sites, against 719 ± 60 and 678 ± 39). On the synthetic map, the per-claim form and the quantile path cost the same within their spread (338 ± 26 and 334 ± 26 of 2118), and the priority path almost twice as much (628 ± 61). No form is the least expensive on all three datasets.

The priority path admitted the interest claim first: on NaCl and the synthetic map every skip was made as uninteresting, while on IR an intensity cap left the rest of the budget to interpolation.

A rule that breaks the recipe loses its guarantee. The sequential rule of the original IR analysis chooses the interest threshold on the calibration draw and then the interpolation threshold on the calibration sites left uncovered, from the remaining budget. On a population with uninformative scores (399 sites; interest and failure labels at rates 0.5 and 0.1), it exceeded $R_E = 0.10$ in 1150 of 4000 draws, a frequency of 0.288 against 0.25 allowed (Monte Carlo standard error 0.007). The priority path expresses the same preference with the order fixed in advance and exceeded the bound in 819 of 4000 draws; the quantile path did so in 832 (Fig. 6, right).

## 5.3 The model sets the cost

A guarantee is of use only if it holds whichever model proposes the skips, and its cost then shows what the model is worth. The quantile path was run with four interpolation protocols on each dataset at the nominal guarantees of Section 5.2: natural-neighbour interpolation with the logistic score, and Gaussian-process interpolation with the posterior standard deviation as score and three rules for the length scale (Section 4.3). At the original IR tolerance almost every site is interpolable and the interest claim alone sets the cost; at the tight tolerance interpolation decides.

Every guarantee held (Fig. 7, lower row). In the order listed, the shared rate exceeded its budget in 49, 49, 49 and 1 of 1000 level checks on IR at the original tolerance, in 193, 179, 191 and 192 of 1000 at the tight tolerance (25 % allowed), in 112, 119, 111 and 123 of 1000 on NaCl, and in 161, 156, 186 and 161 of 1500 on the synthetic map (20 % allowed).

The cost depends on the protocol and on the data (Fig. 7, upper row; Table S3.2). At the original IR tolerance the four protocols measured 295 ± 53, 273 ± 31, 281 ± 34 and 291 ± 41 of 959 sites, and their skipped sets overlapped by about 90 % (Jaccard index 0.90 to 0.92). At the tight tolerance they measured 621 ± 70, 747 ± 56, 753 ± 52 and 799 ± 40, and the Gaussian-process protocols shared a quarter to a third of their skips with natural-neighbour interpolation (Jaccard index 0.27 to 0.33). On NaCl, the Gaussian-process protocols measured 745 to 777 of 1071 sites, against 614 ± 56. On the synthetic map they measured between 1040 ± 202 and 1443 ± 133 of 2118 sites, against 334 ± 26. The difference arises in the calibration draws: more failing calibration sites admit fewer skips at the same budget, and the guarantee is unchanged.

The three length-scale rules give visibly different reasons for measuring a site (Fig. S1; SI 1).

A campaign without certification sets the comparison (Fig. 1; SI 7). On the NaCl scan, a Gaussian process refitted after each level skipped a site when it was sure the line was absent, or when its 68 % interval on the width lay within the tolerance. It measured 658 of 1071 sites and skipped 10 % of the certified-level sites wrongly, inside the spread of the certified campaigns, which measured 614 ± 56 sites with natural-neighbour interpolation and 745 to 777 with the Gaussian process as predictor. Certifying the Gaussian process cost about 100 more measurements and bought the statement: the uncertified rate went unstated and fell unevenly, 18 % on the last level, and intervals of 90 % to 99 % allowed 21 to 31 predictable skips, of which 29 % to 32 % were wrong against the 1 % to 10 % the intervals imply.

## 5.4 Every action certified

A guarantee on rates becomes useful at an instrument once every site of a map carries its reason. Figure 8 shows one campaign per dataset under the quantile path with natural-neighbour interpolation, with every design site marked by the reason recorded for it and every wrong skip found against the fully measured truth.

In the campaigns shown, the wrong skips were 2.3 % of the sites on certified levels of the IR map, against a budget of 0.10 per level, and 13.3 % on NaCl and 10.3 % on the synthetic map, against 0.20. The wrong skips of the synthetic map gather in two compact regions of the composition triangle; the guarantee bounds their rate on each level and leaves their location open (Section 5.5).

The map bound states, from the calibration draw of a level, how many of its sites the map built from coarser levels predicts outside tolerance. Over the 250 campaigns of each configuration it held on 100 % of the certified levels for IR, 88.5 % for NaCl and 90.4 % for the synthetic map, against confidences of 0.75, 0.80 and 0.80. On NaCl about half of the last-level sites lie outside that tolerance; the skip guarantee concerns only the sites a certificate covers.

The record also shows whether a measurement was needed, that is, whether every claim would have skipped the site wrongly (SI 8). Among sites measured because no claim covered them, 45 % were needed on the synthetic map against 10 % of sites drawn at random, and 47 % against 35 % on NaCl, where the width scatters from site to site by about the tolerance.

## 5.5 The claim decides what is protected

A guarantee covers the claim it is given, so the choice of claim needs its own record. Six acceptance metrics were compared on the three maps with the interpolation certificate alone and 50 campaigns per metric: peak or line descriptors with absolute or relative tolerance, two forms of profile envelope, and the error in a basis of the seed spectra, plain or with near-duplicate spectra merged (SI 6). Every metric kept its guarantee. Over 32 combinations of metric, dataset and tolerance, the largest ratio of observed to allowed exceedances was 1.37, with a one-sided probability of 0.04 under the picked levels; all other ratios were at most 1.08.

The metric set the cost. At tolerances a practitioner might pick, the share of certified-level sites that a prediction fails ranged from 1 % to 85 %, and the IR map needed between 233 and 810 measurements. Scaling every tolerance so that 15 % of the sites fail, a control that uses the truth, brought the costs close: 338–453 sites on IR, 287–367 on NaCl and 398–461 on the synthetic map.

At equal difficulty the metric still decided where measurements go (Fig. 9). On the synthetic map the peak claim measured where the peak position and width drift with composition, in the micellar phase and in a band of the emulsion. The profile claim measured along the phase boundaries: 45–46 % of its measurements fell on the 8.8 % of sites next to another phase, against 32 % for the peak claim. The two claims placed 31 % of their measurements on the same sites, and 16 % at the default tolerances.

The metric also decided where wrong skips fall. On IR, the metrics with an absolute term put 72–83 % of their wrong skips on sites with signal, which are 30 % of the skipped sites; the relative profile put them in proportion. On NaCl, the line-width claim put 6 % of its wrong skips on sites holding the (200) line, which are 66 % of the skipped sites. Under every metric the wrong skips clustered, with another wrong skip among the six nearest neighbours 1.5 to 3.0 times as often as for wrong skips placed at random among the skipped sites. The rate also counts wrong skips over all sites of a level. On the synthetic map, where a third of the sites are of interest, 35 % of the interesting sites on certified levels were skipped in error while every level met its budget (SI 6.2).
