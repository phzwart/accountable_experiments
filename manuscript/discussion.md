## Building other guarantees

The forms reported here address three questions about wrong skips, and an instrument may face others. A bound per claim answers how often each reason for skipping failed; a bound on the shared event, how often any skip failed. Other needs call for other forms: unequal budgets when one claim is riskier than the other, a bound on the proportion of false skips among the skipped sites [Jin2023], or a path learned on part of the calibration draw [LauferGoldshtein2023]. Each follows the recipe of Section 3.4 and is accepted by the same Monte Carlo check (SI 4). The cost of a form on a given dataset is hard to predict in advance; Section 5.2 shows that the ordering of three forms changes between datasets. Running candidate forms on a fully measured pilot scan, as done here retrospectively, gives that cost before beam time is committed.

## What is certified shapes the campaign

A guarantee is stated for one acceptance metric, and the choice of that metric deserves the same care as the budget. The bound holds for any metric fixed before the calibration draw: across six metrics on three datasets, no metric exceeded its allowance beyond chance (SI 6). The campaign around the bound changes with the metric. At tolerances a practitioner might pick, the share of sites a prediction fails ranged from 1 % to 85 %, and the measurements on one map from 233 to 821. The metric also decides where the measurements go. On the synthetic map, a peak claim sent them to bands where the peak position and width drift with composition, and a profile claim sent them to phase boundaries; the two placed only 16–31 % of their measurements on the same sites. At tolerances matched to equal difficulty the costs drew close, and the metrics differed in where wrong skips fall: a profile with an absolute term placed most of them on sites with signal, a relative profile spread them evenly, and a line-width claim placed them where the line is weak or absent. Under every metric the wrong skips clustered in space, since interpolation errors do, and a rate guarantee leaves their location free. The metric is therefore best fixed from the scientific question before beam time, stated with the guarantee, and checked on a pilot scan for the cost it implies. A basis that counts each spectral shape once, whatever its abundance, changed little on these data, where the seed designs already sample the map evenly.

## Optimisation and distribution shift

Mapping lends itself to this construction because the population of interest is fixed before the experiment. Every design site is a site the map must cover, and a uniform draw from a level represents that level. Optimisation changes the picture. A Bayesian optimiser or a self-driving laboratory proposes its next experiments from the model it has just fitted, so the points it asks about are chosen by the data, and calibration points from earlier rounds are no longer exchangeable with them. This feedback covariate shift is characteristic of active learning, adaptive experimental design and Bayesian optimisation [Fannjiang2022]. Three routes keep a guarantee in that setting. When the selection mechanism is known, as it is to the optimiser that made the choice, calibration points can be reweighted by the likelihood ratio between proposed and calibration points [Tibshirani2019; Fannjiang2022; Barber2023]; the bound widens wherever the optimiser concentrates on regions the calibration sample seldom visits. A fixed candidate pool, audited by a uniform draw as in this work, restores exchangeability for statements about the pool while the optimiser chooses freely where to look; the statement then concerns the pool, which need not contain what the optimiser proposes next. Online methods control the long-run error rate without exchangeability and give up the finite-sample statement at each step [Gibbs2021]. This work covers mapping, with a fixed population and a uniform audit; optimisation is the natural next case.

## Level sets as a bridge to optimisation

Optimisation can borrow the mapping construction by mapping level sets. A search for the maximum of a response becomes a sequence of mapping problems, each asking where the response exceeds a threshold $q_t$. The interest certificate of this work already makes that claim: a site skipped as uninteresting is a site asserted to lie below the threshold. At stage $t$, the threshold is retargeted to a quantile of the responses measured so far, and the population is the set of design sites of the next level that lie in the region not yet eliminated. Both are fixed by the history of the campaign before the stage's audit is drawn, so conditions (A1)–(A6) of Appendix A hold stage by stage, and each stage carries its own bound on the fraction of its population eliminated in error. The hierarchical design supplies the finer sites where the surviving region needs them, and the sequence of retargeted quantiles plays the part of the adaptive levels in multilevel splitting for rare events [CerouGuyader2007] and of the thresholds in active level-set estimation [Gotovos2013]. The resulting statement concerns the level sets: with probability at least one minus the summed failure probabilities, every stage eliminated at most the stated fraction of sites that lay above its threshold. A bound on the share of the top level set retained, which is closer to what an optimiser needs, follows from the same audits with a lower confidence bound on the size of that set. The location of the single maximum lies beyond a rate statement of this kind, since a bounded fraction of wrong eliminations may include it.
