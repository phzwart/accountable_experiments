# Abstract (draft 3)

*Working title: Accountable experiments: autonomy under contract*

An autonomous experiment decides where to measure and where not to. Each measurement spends limited time and sample, and each skipped site leaves a known unknown that usually cannot be revisited. Delegating those decisions requires every one of them to be justified when it is taken, the skips with a risk bounded by a check the steering model does not control. A model that steers a campaign also chooses the data on which its own uncertainty is judged, so its stated error rate cannot be verified inside the loop it controls, and an audit after the campaign reports once the decisions are spent. An accountable experiment runs under a contract fixed before it starts: a plan of sites, the claims under which a site may be skipped, and a budget on wrong skips with a stated confidence. Before each decision, a uniform calibration draw, made independently of the model, bounds the share of skipped sites that should have been measured. Standard finite-sample results guarantee the bound for any model; the model determines only its cost. On three fully measured datasets, an infrared map, a NaCl microdiffraction scan and a synthetic scattering phase map, levels exceeded their budget no more often than the stated confidence permits, and plans certified for all levels together kept their budget on every level in at least 90 % of campaigns, at 13 % to 41 % more measurements than plans certified level by level. A Gaussian process steering the NaCl scan on its own stated error met its budget at 467 measurements, a fact only the full scan could show; with the same process inside the contract, the bound was known before each skip, at 761. A held-out audit gives the finished map its own bound, as the free R value does for a crystal structure, and every decision can be replayed from the record.
