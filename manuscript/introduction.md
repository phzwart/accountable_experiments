# 1 Introduction (draft 2)

Autonomous experiments now decide where to measure and where not to. At synchrotron beamlines, Gaussian-process surrogates choose the next sample position or composition, and measurement, analysis and decision run as one closed loop [Noack2019; Noack2020; Yager2023]. Self-driving laboratories search formulation and composition spaces in the same way [Burger2020; Nazeri2026], and an autonomous laboratory reported 41 of 58 target inorganic compounds synthesised in 17 days [Szymanski2023], a claim its analysis of the products has since put in dispute [Leeman2024]. Probabilistic models recommend which strains to build next [Radivojevic2020], and laboratory automation keeps lowering the cost of handing experimental choices to machines [Tom2024]. Each decision to measure is also a decision to leave something unmeasured: a closed-loop scattering study mapped the phases of a ternary polymer blend from 65 of 5151 grid compositions, so that 98.7 % of its map is prediction [Upreti2026].

Handing these decisions to a model requires each one to be justified when it is taken. A measurement spends beam time, dose, reagent or sample, and a skipped site leaves a known unknown: once the sample has aged, the beam time has ended or the reagent is spent, it usually cannot be revisited. Guidance on AI risk asks for traceable decisions and documented responsibility [NIST2023], regulation of high-risk systems requires automatic records of their operation [EU2024, Art. 12], and audit frameworks ask for evidence that can be checked against stated expectations [Raji2020]. Accountability is used here in that sense, as a contract: before a decision is taken, a bound is attached to it, and the record allows the decision to be checked against the bound. An account given after the campaign, an audit of the finished map or a report of its error, describes decisions already spent. For a mapping campaign the consequential decision is the skip, and the bound to attach is on how often skips are wrong.

The model steering a campaign cannot supply that bound itself. It chooses the sites it is fitted on, and those are the only sites on which its uncertainty could be judged; selecting and evaluating on the same data is circular [Kriegeskorte2009], whatever the model. A coherent Bayesian model expects to be calibrated [Dawid1982], cross-validation reuses the sites the model selected [Fannjiang2022], and an error found afterwards is laid at the door of the kernel, the hyperparameters or the noise model, each a choice that could have been made otherwise. Figure 1 follows one Gaussian process on a fully measured diffraction scan. Steering on its own stated error, the process stopped after 467 measurements on average with 14.6 % of the sites beyond its seed predicted wrongly, inside its budget of 20 %; during the run nothing showed whether the statement held, and on another map the same kind of statement proved too low (Section 5.2). With the same process as predictor and score inside a contract, each level's bound was fixed before any of its sites was skipped, and the plan measured 761 sites on average.

Upfront contracts are established practice wherever a decision cannot wait for hindsight. Acceptance sampling fixes the sample size and the allowed number of defects before a lot is inspected, so that the risk of accepting a bad lot is known at acceptance [DodgeRomig1959]. Group sequential clinical trials fix in advance how the type I error is spent over interim analyses, so that a trial may stop early with its error rate intact [LanDeMets1983], and registered reports fix hypotheses and analyses before data are collected [Chambers2013]. Design-based survey sampling places the randomness in a plan fixed in advance, so that statements about a finite population hold whatever its values [Neyman1934; Cochran1977], and crystallography keeps a free set of reflections out of refinement so that a model is judged on data it did not fit [Brunger1992]. A mapping campaign fits this pattern closely: its levels are interim analyses, its failure probability is spent across them, and each level is a lot accepted from a sample.

This paper places an autonomous mapping campaign under such a contract. Fixed before the campaign, the contract states the plan, the sites the experiment would measure if none could be skipped, at the finest spacing sought; the claims under which a site may be skipped, that it holds nothing of interest or that its signal, or a quantity derived from it, can be predicted within tolerance; and a budget ε on the share of sites skipped under a false claim, held with confidence c. The plan is divided into nested levels, and the coarsest are measured in full. At each finer level a model fitted to the measured sites is frozen and ranks the sites by how safe a skip is, a uniform sample of the level is measured for calibration, and its labels set how far the skips may go. The bound attaches to the decision on the level before any of its sites is skipped, and holds for any model and score that respect the order fit, freeze, draw, calibrate, decide; the model sets only how many measurements the guarantee costs. The bound is probably approximately correct (PAC) [Valiant1984]: the share of a level skipped under a false claim stays below ε in at least a fraction c of campaigns, so that at ε = 0.20 and c = 0.80 at most a fifth of the level is skipped wrongly at least four times in five. A campaign is run once, and a PAC bound speaks to it, the confidence stating how often a campaign like it keeps its budget; a bound on the expected share holds on average over campaigns, and single levels then exceed the budget far more often (Section 5.1). The statistics are standard, finite-sample risk control whose only assumption is a calibration sample exchangeable with the population it speaks for [Vovk2005; Bates2021; Angelopoulos2025]. A campaign strains that assumption, since every decision changes the data that calibrate the next, and fixing each level before its draw restores it; the contribution is posing the experiment so that the standard results apply inside a closed loop. A held-out audit after the last level gives the finished map a bound of its own, the free set of the campaign.

The evidence comes from three fully measured datasets, an infrared map of a bioreef sample, a NaCl microdiffraction scan [ALSNaCl] and a synthetic small-angle scattering phase map, on which every skip can be checked. Levels exceeded their budgets no more often than the confidence permits, plans certified for all levels together kept their budget on every level in at least 90 % of campaigns at 13 % to 41 % more measurements, and the model and its score set the cost. The Gaussian process of Fig. 1 serves as an illustration: the guarantee rests on the draw alone, and claims about the process concern this process on these maps. A live campaign remains to be run.
